Q4.1Elastic Limit

The elastic limit is the maximum stress up to which a body completely regains its original shape and size on removal of the deforming force.

Q4.2Hardness

Hardness is the ability of a material to resist scratching, indentation or abrasion on its surface.

Q4.3Friction

Static friction adjusts its magnitude to equal the applied force, up to a maximum (limiting) value $f_s\le\mu_sN$; hence it is self-adjusting.

Q5.1Elasticity vs Plasticity

Elasticity: property by which a body regains its original shape/size after the deforming force is removed (e.g. steel, spring). Plasticity: property by which a body does not regain its original shape and stays permanently deformed (e.g. clay, putty).

Q5.2Hooke’s Law

Within the elastic limit, stress is directly proportional to strain: $\text{stress}\propto\text{strain}$, i.e. $\text{stress}=E\times\text{strain}$, where $E$ is the modulus of elasticity, a constant of the material. For a wire, $F/A=Y\,\Delta L/L$.

Q5.3Types of Friction

Static: acts between surfaces at rest relative to each other; self-adjusting, with maximum value $\mu_sN$. Kinetic (sliding): acts when surfaces slide; $f_k=\mu_kN$, with $\mu_k<\mu_s$. Rolling: acts when a body rolls over a surface; much smaller than sliding friction.

Q5.4Stress and Strain

Stress = restoring force per unit area = $F/A$; SI unit N/m² (Pa). Strain = fractional change in dimension (e.g. $\Delta L/L$, $\Delta V/V$); it is a ratio and has no unit.

Q5.5Coefficient of Friction

It depends on (i) the nature (material and roughness) of the two surfaces in contact, and (ii) their condition — e.g. lubrication, cleanliness, temperature. It does not depend on the area of contact or on the normal reaction.

Q6.1Stress–Strain Curve

Plot stress (y-axis) against strain (x-axis).

OA: straight line — proportional region; Hooke’s law holds, A is the proportional limit. AB: stress and strain no longer proportional but the wire still returns to its original length; B is the elastic limit (yield point). BC: beyond B there is permanent (plastic) deformation; the wire does not recover. CD: the material yields with a large strain for little increase in stress; D is the ultimate tensile strength (maximum stress). DE: the wire thins at a neck and breaks at E (fracture/breaking point).

Q6.2Moduli

Young’s modulus $Y=\dfrac{F/A}{\Delta L/L}$: tensile/compressive stress ÷ longitudinal strain; solids only. Bulk modulus $K=\dfrac{-\Delta P}{\Delta V/V}$: volume stress ÷ volumetric strain; solids, liquids and gases. Modulus of rigidity $\eta=\dfrac{F/A}{\theta}$: shear stress ÷ shear strain; solids only. All have SI unit N/m² and dimensions $[ML^{-1}T^{-2}]$.

Q6.3Strain Energy

Let a wire (length $L$, area $A$) be stretched by $l$. Force at extension $x$: $F=\dfrac{YA}{L}x$. Work done for a small extension $dx$: $dW=F\,dx$.

$W=\displaystyle\int_0^{l}\dfrac{YA}{L}x\,dx=\dfrac{YA\,l^2}{2L}=\dfrac12\,F\,l$.

So the elastic potential energy $U=\tfrac12 Fl$. Volume $=AL$, so energy per unit volume $=\dfrac{\tfrac12Fl}{AL}=\tfrac12\,\dfrac FA\cdot\dfrac lL=\tfrac12\times\text{stress}\times\text{strain}=\dfrac{Y}{2}(\text{strain})^2$.

Q7.1 (a)Longitudinal Strain

Q7.2 (a)Young’s Modulus

$A=\pi r^2=3.142\times10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$; $\Delta L=0.25\,\text{m}$.

$Y=\dfrac{FL}{A\,\Delta L}=\dfrac{800\times125}{3.142\times10^{-6}\times0.25}\approx1.27\times10^{11}\,\text{N/m}^2$.

Q7.2 (b)Bulk Modulus

$K=\dfrac{\Delta P\,V}{\Delta V}\Rightarrow\Delta V=\dfrac{\Delta P\,V}{K}=\dfrac{2\times10^{7}\times0.31}{6.1\times10^{10}}\approx1.02\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}^3$.
